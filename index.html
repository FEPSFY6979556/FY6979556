How can seatbelts save lives and cause injury?

Abstract

Seatbelts are an important vehicle safety feature designed to protect occupants during road traffic collisions. This article examines how seatbelts reduce injury risk by restraining occupants and distributing crash forces across stronger areas of the body. It also explores how seatbelt-related injuries can occur, including Seatbelt Syndrome and submarining, as a result of the forces required to restrain occupants during a collision. The importance of correct seatbelt positioning and modern restraint technologies is also discussed. Although seatbelt-related injuries may occur, they are generally less severe than the injuries sustained by unrestrained occupants. Overall, seatbelts remain one of the most effective vehicle safety devices for reducing serious injuries and fatalities.

Introduction

Seatbelts are widely regarded as one of the most effective vehicle safety devices for reducing fatalities and injuries in road traffic collisions (Soica and Gheorghe, 2025). Research has shown that approximately half of passenger vehicle occupants killed in crashes in the United States were not wearing a seatbelt. Seatbelts restrain occupants within their seats during a collision, helping to prevent ejection and reducing the risk of severe injury or death (Rezapour and Ksaibati, 2022). However, despite their ability to save lives, seatbelts can still cause injuries due to the forces required to stop the body from moving forward (Kent et al., 2008). This article explores how seatbelts protect occupants, the causes of seatbelt-related injuries, and the importance of proper seatbelt use in reducing injury risk.

Analysis and discussion

2.1 How seatbelts protect occupants

The seatbelt is a restraint system designed to limit occupant movement when a vehicle experiences sudden deceleration, such as during a collision. During a crash, the vehicle may come to an immediate stop; however, occupants continue moving forward due to inertia. According to Newton's First Law of Motion, an object in motion will remain in motion unless acted upon by an external force. The seatbelt provides this restraining force, counteracting the occupant's forward motion and helping to prevent contact with the vehicle interior or ejection from the vehicle. The modern three-point seatbelt distributes crash forces across the chest and pelvis, which are stronger areas of the body that can withstand greater loads than softer regions such as the abdomen (see Figure 1). Modern restraint systems also incorporate pretensioners, which remove slack from the seatbelt during a collision. By keeping the seatbelt firmly against the body, pretensioners improve occupant restraint and enhance overall protection (Soica and Gheorghe, 2025).

2.2 Why Seatbelt Injuries Occur

Although seatbelts are designed to protect occupants during a collision, injuries can still occur because significant restraining forces are required to stop the body's forward motion (Kent et al., 2008). When these forces are transferred through the seatbelt and into the body, minor injuries such as bruising and friction burns may occur. In more severe collisions, higher impact forces can cause rib fractures, abdominal trauma and spinal injuries (Kent et al., 2008); Alkhaldi et al., 2024). Common seatbelt-related injuries and their causes are summarised in Table 1. One of the more serious outcomes is Seatbelt Syndrome, a pattern of injuries associated with the loading of the seatbelt during a crash ( Alkhaldi et al., 2024). Injury severity is influenced by several factors, including crash speed, occupant posture and submarining, where an occupant slides beneath the lap belt during a collision ( Thorbole, 2015). As crash severity increases, larger forces are required to restrain the occupant, increasing the likelihood and severity of injury (Kent et al., 2008). Bruising Seatbelt Loading Minor
Friction burns Belt movement against skin Minor
Rib fractures High restraint forces Moderate-Severe
Seatbelt Syndrome Compression of abdomen and spine Severe
Submarining injuries Sliding beneath lap belt Severe

2.3 Proper Seatbelt Use and Injury Prevention

The risk of seatbelt-related injuries can be reduced through correct seatbelt positioning and proper occupant posture. The lap belt should be positioned across the pelvis rather than the abdomen, as the pelvis is capable of withstanding greater impact forces and helps to reduce the risk of injury during a collision (Thorbole, 2015). In contrast, the abdomen contains soft tissues and vital organs that are more vulnerable to injury when subjected to seatbelt loading (Kent et al., 2008). The shoulder belt should cross the centre of the chest and shoulder to distribute crash forces more effectively across the upper body. Placing the shoulder belt behind the back or underneath the arm reduces its effectiveness and may increase injury risk. Occupants should also maintain an upright seating position, as leaning forward can reduce the ability of the restraint system to control occupant movement during a collision. Modern seatbelt systems use pretensioners to remove slack and improve occupant restraint, helping to minimise movement and reduce injury severity ( Soica and Gheorghe, 2025).

2.4 Discussion

Though seatbelt-related injuries can occur, they are generally less severe than those sustained by unrestrained occupants (Rezapour and Ksaibati, 2022). These injuries result from the restraining forces required to stop the body during a collision (Kent et al., 2008). However, those same forces prevent occupants from striking the vehicle interior or being ejected from the vehicle (Rezapour and Ksaibati, 2022). Correct seatbelt positioning and modern technologies such as pretensioners can further reduce injury risk (Soica and Gheorghe, 2025). Therefore, despite their limitations, seatbelts remain one of the most effective safety devices in modern vehicles (Rezapour and Ksaibati, 2022; Soica and Gheorghe, 2025).
